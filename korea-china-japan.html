Korea, China & Japan한국 · 중국 · 일본

Korea sits right between two giants. For 2,000 years the three neighbours traded, taught each other, fought wars and made peace. Here are the big moments, and the places on our trip where you can still see them.

Part 1

The shrimp between whales

고래 싸움에 새우 등 터진다

"When whales fight, the shrimp's back breaks." Koreans often use this proverb to describe their country's history. A small peninsula next to huge powers often got caught in their fights, and many of the wars below were really about the bigger countries reaching each other through Korea.

Part 2

2,000 years in 14 moments

108 BCEKRCN

China's Han empire moves in

China's Han dynasty conquered the early Korean kingdom of Gojoseon and set up military districts in the north of the peninsula. Along with soldiers came Chinese writing, iron tools and new ideas.

612KRCN

The great ambush at the Salsu River

China's Sui dynasty sent one of the biggest armies the world had seen to conquer the Korean kingdom of Goguryeo. General Eulji Mundeok lured it deep into Korea, then trapped it at the Salsu River. Old records say only a few thousand of the hundreds of thousands of soldiers made it home. The defeat helped bring down the Sui dynasty.

660–676KRCNJP

Allies, then enemies

The Korean kingdom of Silla teamed up with China's Tang dynasty to defeat its rivals Baekje and Goguryeo. Japan sent a fleet to help its ally Baekje, and lost at the Battle of Baekgang in 663. Then Tang tried to take over the whole peninsula, so Silla turned on its former ally and drove the Tang armies out by 676.

500s–700sKRCNJP

Not just wars: a bridge for ideas

Buddhism, Chinese characters and Confucian ideas travelled from China through Korea to Japan. Scholars, monks, builders and artists from the Korean kingdom of Baekje helped shape early Japanese temples, writing and art.

1231–1273KRMN

The Mongols, and Jeju's last stand

The Mongol Empire invaded Goryeo Korea again and again for decades. A group of Korean soldiers called the Sambyeolcho refused to surrender and made their final stand on Jeju, where they were defeated in 1273. The Mongols then used Jeju as a horse ranch.

See it on our trip: Jeju's small horses descend from those Mongol herds.
1274 & 1281MNKRJP

The "divine wind"

The Mongols forced Korea to build ships and send soldiers to invade Japan, twice. Both times, storms wrecked much of the fleet. The Japanese called the storms kamikaze, "divine wind," the same word used much later for suicide pilots in World War II.

1392–1800sKRCN

The "big brother" system

Joseon Korea sent regular missions with gifts to China's emperors and accepted China as the senior partner. In return, Korea ran its own affairs, traded and got protection. It was more like a ceremony of respect between neighbours than China ruling Korea.

1592–1598JPKRCN

The Imjin War

Japan's ruler Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded Korea, planning to march on through to conquer China. Seoul fell within weeks and the king fled. Korea fought back with Admiral Yi Sun-sin's navy, volunteer "righteous armies" and even warrior monks, and China's Ming dynasty sent armies to help. After six years and terrible destruction, Japan withdrew when Hideyoshi died.

Japanese forces also carried off thousands of Korean craftspeople. Captured potters started Japan's famous porcelain industry.

See it on our trip: Gyeongbokgung Palace burned during this war and lay in ruins for about 270 years (Mon 2 Nov). Admiral Yi's statue stands on Gwanghwamun Square.
1607–1811KRJP

Making peace

After the war, Joseon sent 12 large goodwill missions to Japan. Crowds lined the streets to see the Korean envoys, and Japanese scholars and artists swapped poems and paintings with them. For about 200 years, the two countries were at peace.

1636–1637MNKR

The king who had to bow

The Manchus, who were about to conquer China and found the Qing dynasty, invaded Korea. King Injo held out in a mountain fortress through a freezing winter, then surrendered. He had to kneel three times and touch his head to the ground nine times before the Manchu emperor at a place called Samjeondo. Koreans remember it as one of their most humiliating moments.

See it on our trip: the Samjeondo stone monument stands at Seokchon Lake, right next to Lotte World (Thu 5 Nov).
1876–1895JPCNKR

Two empires fight over Korea

Japan, newly modernised, forced Korea to open its ports in 1876. In 1894–95, China and Japan fought the First Sino-Japanese War, largely over who would control Korea. Japan won. In 1895, Japanese agents broke into Gyeongbokgung and killed Queen Min, who had been working against Japanese influence.

See it on our trip: the queen was killed in the palace grounds at Gyeongbokgung.
1910–1945JPKR

Japanese rule

Japan made Korea a colony in 1910. Over 35 years, Koreans lost control of their country. Later in the period, schools banned the Korean language, Koreans were pressured to take Japanese names, and many were forced to work in mines and factories or fight in Japan's army. Women and girls were forced into sexual slavery for Japanese soldiers; they are known as "comfort women." On 1 March 1919, millions of Koreans joined peaceful protests for independence, and a Korean government-in-exile was set up in Shanghai, China.

See it on our trip: 15 August, the day Japan surrendered in 1945, is Korea's Liberation Day, Gwangbokjeol, "the day the light returned."
1950–1953KRCN

The Korean War

After 1945 Korea was split in two. In 1950 North Korea invaded the South. A UN force led by the United States came to help the South, and when it pushed close to China's border, China sent hundreds of thousands of soldiers to fight for the North. The war ended in 1953 with a ceasefire, not a peace treaty. South Korea and China didn't set up normal relations until 1992.

See it on our trip: the DMZ, the border that still divides the peninsula (Thu 5 Nov, if you go).
1965 → todayKRCNJP

Rivals and partners

South Korea and Japan restored diplomatic ties in 1965, and South Korea and China in 1992. Today the three are some of each other's biggest trading partners, and young people in all three countries watch each other's dramas, anime, K-pop and films.

Part 3

Heroes Koreans remember

Eulji Mundeok

The Goguryeo general who trapped the Sui army in 612. Euljiro, the street you'll visit on Mon 2 Nov, is named after him.

Admiral Yi Sun-sin

Never lost a sea battle. At Myeongnyang in 1597 he beat a Japanese fleet of over 130 ships with just 13. He was killed in the war's final battle in 1598 and told his men to keep his death secret until they won.

Yu Gwan-sun

A 17-year-old student who helped lead the 1919 independence protests. She was imprisoned and died in prison in 1920 at 17, and is remembered as a symbol of courage.

Part 4

Why it still matters

History is still a live topic between the three countries. People in each country often see these issues differently, so it's worth hearing more than one side.

Apologies and memory

Japanese leaders have apologised for the colonial era several times. Many Koreans feel the apologies haven't gone far enough, especially for the "comfort women" and forced labourers, while many in Japan feel the issue has been settled by past agreements and payments.

Islands and maps

South Korea controls a small group of rocky islets in the sea between Korea and Japan, which Korea calls Dokdo and Japan calls Takeshima. Both countries claim them.

Whose history?

Chinese and Korean historians have argued over how to describe ancient kingdoms like Goguryeo, which covered parts of today's China and Korea. Each side tends to tell the story as part of its own national history.

Mission

History hunters in Seoul

  1. Find Admiral Yi's statue on Gwanghwamun Square. What's in front of it? (Hint: a model of his famous ship.)
  2. At Gyeongbokgung, find out which buildings are original and which were rebuilt.
  3. If you go to Lotte World, look for the Samjeondo monument at Seokchon Lake next door.
  4. On the DMZ tour, ask the guide how many tunnels from the North have been found.

If you have time in Seoul: the War Memorial of Korea in Yongsan (tanks, planes and ships outside) and Seodaemun Prison History Hall, where independence fighters were jailed.
