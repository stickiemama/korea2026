Our question

How did Korea get rich so fast?

After our DMZ tour and the War Memorial, we wondered: if Korea was so poor after the war, how did it build all this wealth in such a short time? Here is the story, decade by decade.

Part 2

Decade by decade

1950s

Starting from rubble

When the Korean War ended in 1953, Seoul was in ruins, the industrial north was cut off, and income was under about US$100 per person a year, poorer than many countries in Africa. American aid kept people fed.

Two things quietly prepared the ground: land reform, which gave farmland to the farmers who worked it, and a hunger for education. Parents sacrificed almost anything so their children could study.

1960s

Wigs, shirts and shoes

From 1961, the government under Park Chung-hee ran the economy with five-year plans and one big goal: export. Factories made cheap goods for the world, with young workers, many of them teenage girls, working very long hours. Wigs made from human hair were among Korea's top exports in the 1960s!

Thousands of Korean nurses and coal miners also went to work in West Germany and sent their wages home.

1970s

Steel and ships

Next came heavy industry: steel, ships, cars and chemicals. The POSCO steelworks was partly paid for with money from Japan, after the two countries restored relations in 1965.

Hyundai's founder, Chung Ju-yung, wanted to build giant ships but had no shipyard. He went to British bankers with a photo of an empty beach and a 500-won banknote showing Admiral Yi's turtle ship, as proof that Koreans had built great ships before. He won a Greek order for two huge tankers, then built the shipyard and the ships at the same time.

1980s–90s

Chips, democracy and the Olympics

Korea moved into electronics and computer memory chips, and Samsung became one of the world's biggest chipmakers. In 1987, huge protests won full democracy, and in 1988 Seoul hosted the Olympics, showing the world a modern country.

1997–98

The gold campaign

In the 1997 Asian financial crisis, Korea ran short of foreign money and needed an IMF bailout. In early 1998, about 3.5 million people queued to donate their gold rings, necklaces and medals, about 225 tonnes in all, to help repay the debt. Korea paid back the IMF years ahead of schedule.

Today

From receiving aid to giving it

In 2010 Korea joined the club of major aid-giving countries, often described as the first country to go from receiving aid to giving it. Today Korean phones, cars, ships, chips, K-pop and K-dramas are known around the world.

Part 3

Thinking it through

The costs

Not a miracle for everyone

The growth came under military-led governments. Unions and protests were suppressed, workers faced dangerous conditions, and full democracy came only in 1987. Koreans still argue about how to judge Park Chung-hee: some credit him with ending poverty, others stress the repression.

The big family firms (chaebol) became very powerful, and today Korea faces fierce school competition, long working hours and one of the world's lowest birth rates.

Singapore link

The Four Asian Tigers

South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Singapore were called the "Four Asian Tigers." All four grew rich fast in the same decades by exporting, saving, and investing heavily in education, often with strong governments steering the way.
