History

Tea arrives in Korea

A famous story says that in 828, an envoy brought tea seeds back from Tang China and the king had them planted on Jirisan mountain. Buddhist monks drank tea to stay alert during meditation. Under the Joseon dynasty, tea drinking faded as Buddhism lost royal favour.

Revival

Bringing tea back

Around 1979, the founder of the beauty company Amorepacific began turning rough volcanic land on Jeju into tea fields, hoping to revive Korean tea culture. The Osulloc fields grew from that dream. Jeju's mild climate, rain and mist suit tea plants.

Science

One plant, many teas

Green, black and oolong tea all come from the same plant. The difference is oxidation: green tea leaves are heated quickly to stop them changing, oolong is partly oxidised, and black tea is left to oxidise fully until the leaves darken, like a cut apple turning brown.

Matcha

Shade-grown tea

For powdered green tea, some tea bushes are covered for a few weeks before harvest. Less sunlight makes the leaves greener, sweeter and less bitter. The leaves are then ground into fine powder, which you drink whole, or eat in ice cream.

Try: green tea ice cream or roll cake, and compare it with a cup of plain green tea.
Look closer

A building like a cup

The museum building is said to be inspired by the shape of a teacup. Walk around it and see if you agree, then head out to the tea rows for the view.
