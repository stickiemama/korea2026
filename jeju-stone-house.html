Jeju · Seongsan

Our Jeju Stone House제주 돌집

Jeju's traditional houses are called dol-chip (stone house) or chogajip (thatched house). Here's what makes the one we're sleeping in special.

01Built from volcanoes

Walls with holes on purpose

Jeju is covered in black volcanic rock, so that's what people built with. The stones are stacked with no cement. The gaps let Jeju's strong wind blow through, so the wall doesn't get pushed over.

Try: hold your hand near a gap in the garden wall and feel the air come through.
02Weird but true

The black dragon wall

The same stacked walls surround Jeju's fields. Add them all up and they stretch over 20,000 km, about half the way around the Earth. Locals call them the "black dragon wall."

03History

Roofs tied down with rope nets

Old Jeju houses had low, rounded roofs of thatched grass. Ropes were tied over them in a grid, like a net, so typhoons couldn't rip them off. The houses were built low to hide from the wind too.

Look for: roofs with a rope grid at Seongeup Folk Village.
04Detective challenge

Are our beams really old?

In old houses, the kitchen fire had no chimney. Smoke drifted up through the beams for decades and turned them black, which also kept bugs and rot away. Today some owners keep those old sooty beams, and some paint new wood black to look the same. Investigate:

Probably oldWobbly, uneven logs. Axe marks. Every beam a different thickness. Joints that slot together with no metal.
Probably newSmooth beams all the same size. Perfect paint. Screws or metal brackets.

Make your guess, then ask the host who was right.

05History

The walk to the front door

Houses sat at the end of a winding lane between stone walls, called an olle. The twists slowed the wind and stopped passers-by from seeing in. Jeju's famous Olle hiking trails are named after these lanes.

06Weird but true

Doors? Poles.

No gates and no locks. Families laid wooden poles called jeongnang across the entrance instead. No poles meant "come in." One meant "back soon." Three meant "away for a long time."

07Family life

Grandparents across the yard

A Jeju home was often two houses facing each other across a yard. When a son married, his parents moved into the outer house. Both families lived a few steps apart but cooked and ate separately. That was unusual in Korea and shows how independent Jeju families were.

08Inside

Floors that heat up

Bedrooms had ondol: a fire under the floor sent hot smoke through channels beneath the stones, warming the room from below. That's why Koreans traditionally sat and slept on the floor. Many homes still have underfloor heating today.

Check: is our floor warm?
09Gross but true

The pig toilet

The outhouse, called a dottongsi, sat over a stone pen where the family pigs lived and ate what dropped down. That's where Jeju's black pork story comes from. Don't worry, our bathroom is modern.

10History

Why so few are left

In the 1970s, a national campaign to modernise villages swapped most thatched roofs for slate or tin. Many villages had also been burned during the violence of 1948–49. Seongeup Folk Village, about 25 minutes from Seongsan, still has whole streets of thatched stone houses where people live.
