About the palace

History

The palace that kept coming back

Built in 1395 by the founder of the Joseon dynasty, Gyeongbokgung burned during the Japanese invasion of 1592 and lay in ruins for about 270 years. It was rebuilt in 1867, then mostly torn down under Japanese rule. Restoration has been going on since 1990 and continues today.

Name

"Greatly blessed by heaven"

Gyeongbok means roughly "greatly blessed by heaven." The main hall, Geunjeongjeon, is where kings held ceremonies and received officials.

Our guide said

Five peaks, a sun and a moon

Behind the throne stands a painted screen, Irworobongdo: five mountain peaks under a sun and a moon. The sun stands for the king and the moon for the queen, and the five peaks are often explained as Korea's five great mountains, standing for the whole country.

Today three of those mountains (Baekdu, Kumgang and Myohyang) are in North Korea. Only two, Jirisan and Bukhansan, are in the South. A palace painting that quietly reminds you Korea is still divided.

Spot it: the same screen is on the back of the 10,000 won note.
Colour code

Dancheong: painted armour

The bright patterns under the roofs are called dancheong, painted in five traditional colours. They're beautiful, but they also protected the wood from rain and insects, and showed how important a building was.

Look up

Guardians on the roof

Little clay figures march along the roof ridges. They're called japsang, and represent characters from the Chinese tale Journey to the West, including the Monkey King, set there to scare away evil spirits and fire.

Stone guardian

The haechi

Our guide pointed out a stone monster guarding the palace. It's a haechi, an imaginary lion-dog creature believed to tell right from wrong and to protect against fire, which was very important for a wooden palace that had already burned down once. With its big eyes and fangs, it looks a lot like a tiger.

Seoul mascot: a cute cartoon haechi is the official symbol of Seoul. Look for it on signs and buses.
Tigers of Korea

Once a land of real tigers

Yes, Korea really had tigers! Amur (Siberian) tigers, the biggest cats in the world, once roamed the whole peninsula. Old Joseon records complain about tigers attacking villages, and kings sent special hunters after them.

Hunting wiped them out, especially organised hunts during Japanese rule. The last wild tiger in South Korea is usually said to have been shot in the early 1920s near Gyeongju.

Tigers of Korea

Why tigers are on everything

The tiger is almost Korea's national animal. In the founding myth, a tiger and a bear both tried to become human; the bear won and became the mother of Dangun, the first king. Folk paintings of a goofy tiger with a magpie were hung to bring good luck and keep evil away.

Some Koreans say the peninsula is shaped like a leaping tiger, and the mascots of the 1988 Seoul Olympics (Hodori) and the 2018 PyeongChang Winter Olympics (Soohorang) were both tigers. So tigers on tees and notebooks are a bit like the lion in Singapore.

Shop spotting: look for the magpie-and-tiger painting on souvenirs.
