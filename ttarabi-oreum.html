Part 2

The cards

Famous

Queen of the oreums

Ttarabi is one of Jeju's most loved oreums because of its shape: several craters sit side by side, with soft curving ridges between them. From the top, the ridges look like waves, especially when they're covered in silver grass.

Legend

A family of hills

One local story says the name comes from an old Jeju word for a grandfather or father-in-law figure. The smaller oreums nearby have family names too, such as Moji Oreum ("mother") and Jangja Oreum ("eldest son"), as if Ttarabi were the head of the family.

From the top: try to pick out the rest of the family.
Nature

Silver grass, not reeds

The tall grass is eoksae, silver grass. People often mix it up with reeds (galdae), but reeds grow in wet places by rivers and look brownish, while silver grass grows on dry hillsides and glows silvery-white. Each fluffy head is full of tiny seeds that drift away on the wind.

Careful

Swords with an edge

Silver grass leaves contain silica, the same mineral as sand and glass, and their edges are finely toothed. Run a finger the wrong way along a leaf and it can give you a paper cut. Good for sword fights, as long as you hold the stalk, not the leaf.

Jeju life

Horse country and wind

The Gasi-ri area has long been grazing land, and Jeju horses still roam the pastures around the oreums. Today the windy uplands are also home to rows of wind turbines, part of Jeju's plan to run on clean energy.

Looking after oreums

Why some oreums need a rest

Oreums are made of loose volcanic soil, and so many visitors now climb them that some paths have worn into deep scars. Jeju closes some oreums for a few years at a time to let the grass grow back. Yongnuni, near the rail bike, was one of them. Sticking to the main paths helps keep oreums like Ttarabi open.
