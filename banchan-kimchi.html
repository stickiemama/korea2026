Part 1

Banchan: the little dishes

The basics

Rice, soup and side dishes

A traditional Korean meal is built around rice (bap) and soup (guk), with banchan shared in the middle of the table. In restaurants they usually come free with your main dish, and you can ask for more.

Try saying: banchan deo juseyo, "more side dishes, please."
History

Twelve dishes for the king

In Joseon times, the number of banchan showed your status. Ordinary families might have 3, 5 or 7, while the king's royal table, the surasang, had 12.

Namul

Seasoned vegetables

Many banchan are namul and muchim: vegetables, mushrooms or seaweed blanched or sliced, then tossed with garlic, sesame oil, soy sauce, vinegar or chilli flakes. They're quick to make, so the selection often changes with the seasons.

What we ate

Cold garlicky mushrooms and radish

The cold mushrooms and radish in a mildly spicy, garlicky sauce were most likely a muchim, a dressed side dish. Radish salad in chilli and vinegar is called musaengchae, and seasoned mushrooms are beoseot muchim. Next time, ask the staff its name and add it to the journal.

The mother sauces

Three jars behind every kitchen

Most Korean flavours start from three fermented pastes and sauces: ganjang (soy sauce), doenjang (soybean paste) and gochujang (chilli paste). Families kept them in big clay jars on an outdoor platform called a jangdokdae.

Look for: rows of brown clay jars outside houses and restaurants.
Weird but true

Temple food has no garlic

Korean Buddhist temple cooking leaves out garlic, onion, spring onion, chives and leeks, the "five pungent vegetables," because monks believed they stir up strong emotions. It's still full of flavour, from mushrooms, sesame and fermented sauces.

Part 2

Kimchi: the star

History

Kimchi was not always red

Early kimchi was simply vegetables salted or pickled to last through winter, and a Goryeo poet wrote about pickled radish in the 1200s. Chilli peppers only reached Korea from the Americas, via trade with Japan and Europe, in the late 1500s or early 1600s. Red, spicy kimchi became common in the 1700s, and the napa cabbage kimchi we know spread in the 1800s.

Science

Alive in the jar

Kimchi is fermented. Friendly bacteria (the same family as in yoghurt) feed on the vegetables' sugars and produce a sour, fizzy tang. Fresh kimchi tastes crisp and mild, while old kimchi gets sour and is perfect for stews like kimchi-jjigae.

Heritage

Kimjang: making kimchi together

Every late autumn, families and neighbours gather to make huge batches of kimchi for winter, called kimjang. Grandmothers, aunts and neighbours salt and stuff hundreds of cabbages in a day, then share the results. UNESCO added kimjang to its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2013.

Right now: it's almost kimjang season, so look out for mountains of cabbages at markets.
Then and now

From buried jars to kimchi fridges

Families used to bury kimchi in clay jars (onggi) in the ground, where the temperature stayed cool and steady all winter. Today many Korean homes have a separate kimchi refrigerator designed to copy those conditions.

Many kinds

Not just cabbage

There are over 200 kinds of kimchi: cubed radish (kkakdugi), cucumber (oi-sobagi), spring onion (pa-kimchi), and "water kimchi" (mul-kimchi) in a cool, tangy broth. Regions and families have their own recipes.

Fun fact

Kimchi in space

When Korea's first astronaut, Yi So-yeon, went to the International Space Station in 2008, scientists developed a special space kimchi for her. And Koreans say "kimchi!" when posing for photos, the way English speakers say "cheese."
