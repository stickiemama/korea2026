Part 1

Cousins, not siblings

Our question

Why does Korean sound Chinese?

Walking around Seoul, we kept hearing words that sounded a bit like Hokkien or Cantonese. Was Korean a Chinese dialect? Not at all, but our ears were catching something real.

Families

Different language families

Chinese belongs to the Sino-Tibetan family. Korean belongs to its own small family. Its grammar works completely differently: the verb comes at the end, and little endings are stuck onto words to show who did what, much more like Japanese than Chinese.

Chinese: 我吃饭 (I eat rice). Korean: 나는 밥을 먹어요, "I rice eat."

History

2,000 years of borrowing

For about two thousand years, educated Koreans read and wrote in Chinese characters, called hanja. Along with the characters came thousands of words for ideas, government, science and learning. Today up to about 60% of words in a Korean dictionary are Sino-Korean, though everyday speech uses fewer.

Hangul

An alphabet of its own

In 1443 King Sejong created Hangul, a simple alphabet so that ordinary people could learn to read in days rather than years. Its consonant shapes are said to be based on the shape of the mouth and tongue when making each sound. Hanja are still sometimes used in newspapers, names and on temple signs.

Spot it: look for Chinese characters on palace gates, temples and old signs.
Part 2

Why our ears noticed

Sound clue

Old Chinese endings

Korea borrowed its words from older Chinese, which had syllables ending in -p, -t and -k. Mandarin lost those endings, but Hokkien and Cantonese kept them, so Sino-Korean words often sound closer to southern Chinese than to Mandarin.

Korean changed the -t ending into -l: 一 is il (Cantonese yat), 七 is chil (Cantonese cat) and 八 is pal (Cantonese baat).

Weird but true

Two ways to count

Korean has two sets of numbers! Sino-Korean numbers (il, i, sam, sa) are used for money, dates, phone numbers and minutes. Native Korean numbers (hana, dul, set, net) are used for counting things, ages and hours. So 3:30 is se-si sam-sip-bun: native for the hour, Sino-Korean for the minutes.

Wider family

Japanese and Vietnamese too

Japan and Vietnam also borrowed Chinese words, so the same idea echoes across Asia. "Thank you / gratitude" 感謝 is gamsa in Korean, kansha in Japanese and cảm tạ in Vietnamese.

Pure Korean

The native words

Many of the most everyday words are native Korean with no Chinese link at all: mul (water), bap (rice, meal), eomma (mum), saram (person), haneul (sky). Hangul itself is a native word: han "great" + geul "writing."
