Our visit

A glimpse across the border

On Wed 4 Nov we took a half-day guided tour to the DMZ. Through the binoculars at the observatory we could just make out people cycling into a building in North Korea.

The tour included a Q&A with Kim Hye-kyung, a North Korean defector. She told us hunger was worst in the 1990s, but after the 2000s people who worked had enough to eat, even if they couldn’t get rich. And K-dramas? Too much romance: she prefers American action movies! We bought a signed copy of her book, I Never Wanted to Go to South Korea.

Good to know

Passports, please!

Everyone needs their passport: soldiers check them before the tour bus can enter the military area. Inside the Third Tunnel photos aren't allowed, phones and bags go in lockers, and you wear a hard hat. The walk back up is steep!

Heads-up: the army can close parts of the DMZ at short notice, so the route may change on the day.
The cards

About the DMZ

What is it?

A war that never officially ended

The Korean War (1950–1953) stopped with an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting, on 27 July 1953. No peace treaty was ever signed, so North and South Korea are technically still at war.

The Demilitarized Zone is a 4 km-wide buffer strip running about 250 km across the peninsula, with the Military Demarcation Line, the actual border, down the middle.

Trip link: the Korea, China & Japan page tells how Korea was divided.
Imjingak

Ribbons and a bridge to freedom

At Imjingak, thousands of colourful ribbons tied to the fence carry messages of hope for reunification. Freedom Bridge nearby is where prisoners of war walked home in 1953. There's even a steam locomotive full of bullet holes, left where it was stopped in the war.

Underground

The Third Tunnel

In 1978, South Korea discovered a tunnel dug by North Korea under the border, 73 m below ground and pointed towards Seoul, just 52 km away. It's about 2 m high and wide, enough for thousands of soldiers an hour. North Korea claimed it was a coal mine, and even painted the walls black.

Four tunnels have been found so far, and some people believe there are more.

Tall people: mind your head. The hard hats are there for a reason!
Look north

Dora Observatory

From Dora Observatory you can look across into North Korea, and on a clear day see the city of Kaesong. Look for North Korea's village of Kijong-dong and its giant flagpole, about 160 m tall, built to beat South Korea's taller flagpole across the line in a real-life "flagpole war."

Weird but true

The first station to the North

Dorasan Station is a shiny modern railway station with almost no trains. Its sign says it is "not the last station from the South, but the first station toward the North." It was built in hope that one day trains would run all the way from Busan through North Korea to China and Europe.

Nature

An accidental nature reserve

Because almost no people have entered the DMZ for over 70 years, it has become a refuge for wildlife, including rare red-crowned cranes, which fly in for winter, and Korean water deer, the little deer with vampire-like fangs.

People

Defectors

Around 34,000 North Koreans have escaped to live in the South. Very few cross the DMZ itself, which is too heavily guarded; most make a long, dangerous journey through China and other countries first.

In the South they go through a resettlement centre called Hanawon, where they learn about everyday life, from using cards and apps to the many English words Koreans use that North Koreans have never heard.

History

The Arduous March

In the mid-1990s North Korea suffered a terrible famine, which its government called the "Arduous March." Floods, the collapse of its ally the Soviet Union and a failing farm system left millions without enough food. Estimates of how many people died range from hundreds of thousands to over two million. It's the hunger our defector remembered from the 1990s.

People

Families divided

When the border closed, millions of families were split, with parents, children and siblings on opposite sides who could never visit or even phone each other. Many have died without meeting again. At Imjingak there's an altar where families bow towards their hometowns in the North at Chuseok and Lunar New Year.
